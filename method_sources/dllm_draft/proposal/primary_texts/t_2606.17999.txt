 Abstract 
 
 MDLMs generate text by denoising a preallocated masked response canvas, making response-length modeling central to instruction tuning.
Existing MDLMs often inherit the autoregressive convention of using repeated
 [EOS] tokens for padding during instruction tuning, giving [EOS] a dual role as both a
semantic terminator and a padding token. We show that this dual role is a root
cause of [EOS] overflow under large-block decoding.
To decouple these roles, we propose VoidPadding, which introduces [VOID] 
for padding and reserves [EOS] for termination. During inference, the learned [EOS] signal enables early stopping, while the learned
 [VOID] signal guides adaptive response canvas expansion.
On Dream-7B-Instruct, VoidPadding improves the block-size-averaged four-task mean across mathematical reasoning and code generation benchmarks by + 17.84 +17.84 points over the original model and + 6.95 +6.95 points over RainbowPadding, while reducing decoding NFE by 55.7% on average.
Code is available at https://github.com/Haru-LCY/VoidPadding . 
 
 
 
 1 Introduction 

 
 Masked diffusion language models  4 ; 47 ; 35 ; 34 (MDLMs) offer a promising alternative to autoregressive language models  39 (ARLMs). Whereas ARLMs generate tokens sequentially from left to right, MDLMs use bidirectional attention to denoise the entire sequence in parallel  7 . This bidirectional conditioning, however, makes MDLMs sensitive to the padding tokens introduced in instruction tuning. 
 
 
 During instruction tuning  14 , instruction–response pairs are typically processed in batches for efficiency and padded to the
maximum sequence length within each mini-batch.
Following a convention inherited from ARLM training, MDLMs often use repeated [EOS] tokens as
padding  38 . In ARLMs, causal
masking  ( 46 ) prevents earlier positions from conditioning on
 [EOS] padding, so [EOS] primarily serves as a
semantic terminator. In MDLMs, however, [EOS] padding is also included in the
training target. Thus, [EOS] takes on two
roles: the semantic terminator role and the padding role. 
 
 
 During inference, MDLMs generate on a predefined response canvas whose positions are jointly modeled under bidirectional
attention  12 .
The padding role of [EOS] introduced during instruction tuning therefore offers a length signal: high [EOS] confidence
near the tail indicates that the current canvas may exceed the desired output length  32 ; 53 . However, this signal
becomes fragile under large-block decoding.
Once high-confidence tail [EOS] tokens are generated, they become part of the denoising context and can further increase nearby
 [EOS] probabilities, leading to short effective responses and repeated [EOS] .
This failure mode is
known as [EOS] overflow  ( 28 ) . 
 
 
 Figure 1: Overview of VoidPadding. VoidPadding decouples termination from padding
by using [EOS] only for stopping and [VOID] only for padding.
During inference, [VOID] token generation is banned, decoding stops at the leftmost visible
 [EOS] , and the canvas may be expanded based on tail [VOID] signals. 
 
 
 Based on these insights, we propose VoidPadding , a lightweight
training–inference co-design that introduces [VOID] as a new padding
token and reserves [EOS] solely for semantic termination.
Training sequences are formatted as [prompt] [response] [EOS] [VOID]* ,
where each example contains a single [EOS] followed by
 [VOID] padding.
During inference, [VOID] generation is banned. We introduce
 [EOS] Termination , which stops decoding once no masks remain before the
leftmost visible [EOS] .
This prevents [EOS] overflow and avoids redundant generation after the
effective response.
Additionally, we propose an optional inference-time
 VoidExpansion strategy, which uses the [VOID] 
length signal to expand insufficient canvases before denoising. 
 
 
 Our contributions are: 
 
 1. 
 
 We systematically analyze the mechanism behind [EOS] overflow and
show that it stems from the dual use of [EOS] for termination and
padding during instruction tuning. 
 
 2. 
 
 We propose VoidPadding, a training–inference co-design that decouples
the two roles by using [VOID] for padding and reserving
 [EOS] for termination, enabling [EOS] Termination for
early stopping and VoidExpansion for adaptive canvas expansion. 
 
 3. 
 
 Experiments show that VoidPadding prevents [EOS] overflow,
improves generation quality and decoding efficiency, and remains robust
across block sizes and canvas lengths. 
 
 
 
 
 
 2 Preliminary 

 
 2.1 Masked Diffusion Modeling 

 
 MDLMs generate by denoising discrete
sequences  ( 44 ; 41 ) .
Let 𝒱 = { 1 , … , V } \mathcal{V}=\{1,\ldots,V\} be the token vocabulary, 𝖬 ∉ 𝒱 \mathsf{M}\notin\mathcal{V} 
the mask token, and 𝐳 t ∈ ( 𝒱 ∪ { 𝖬 } ) L \mathbf{z}_{t}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\mathsf{M}\})^{L} the sequence state
at diffusion step t ∈ { 0 , … , T } t\in\{0,\ldots,T\} , where T T is the total number of denoising steps.
Here, t = 0 t=0 denotes clean data and t = T t=T the fully masked sequence.
The masked and visible positions are denoted by ℳ t = { i : z t i = 𝖬 } \mathcal{M}_{t}=\{i:z_{t}^{i}=\mathsf{M}\} and
 ℳ ¯ t = { 1 , … , L } ∖ ℳ t \bar{\mathcal{M}}_{t}=\{1,\ldots,L\}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{t} , respectively. 
 
 
 The forward process gradually corrupts a clean sequence 𝐳 0 ∼ p data \mathbf{z}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}} by independently masking each position over T T steps.
At each step, tokens are progressively replaced by 𝖬 \mathsf{M} according to a fixed schedule, so that 𝐳 T \mathbf{z}_{T} is fully masked.
During training, a timestep t t is sampled and the model predicts the original token at each masked position, yielding the denoising cross-entropy objective
 ℒ ( θ ) = 𝔼 𝐳 0 , t , 𝐳 t [ ∑ i ∈ ℳ t − log p θ ( z 0 i ∣ 𝐳 t ) ] . \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{0},t,\mathbf{z}_{t}}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}-\log p_{\theta}(z_{0}^{i}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t})\right]. 
This objective trains the denoiser on various masking patterns, enabling
 p θ p_{\theta} to predict masked tokens from different visible contexts  ( 30 ) . 
 
 
 MDLM decoding procedures include block diffusion  ( 3 ) and pure
diffusion  ( 56 ) .
Both of them start from a fully masked canvas of length L L with block size B B ,
where pure diffusion corresponds to B = L B=L .
At each step, the model uses bidirectional attention to condition on the whole sequence
and predicts p θ ( ⋅ ∣ 𝐳 t ) p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z}_{t}) for all i ∈ ℳ t i\in\mathcal{M}_{t} in parallel,
so one denoising step corresponds to one model forward pass.
Decoding then proceeds in two steps within the current block.
First, it selects commitment positions, commonly using
confidence  9 or negative entropy scores  ( 6 ) .
Second, it selects tokens for these positions, typically by argmax under
 p θ p_{\theta} .
Decoding moves to the next block once the current block is fully committed and terminates
when all masks have been filled. 
 
 
 
 2.2 Instruction-Tuning Data Construction 

 
 Instruction tuning (IT) trains pretrained language models on prompt–response pairs,
improving instruction following and zero-shot generalization  14 .
For efficient batching, prompt–response sequences are terminated by
 [EOS] and padded to a common length.
MDLM IT often follows the ARLM convention of using [EOS] as the padding token.
Let B = { ( p k , r k ) } k = 1 b B=\{(p_{k},r_{k})\}_{k=1}^{b} be a mini-batch of size b b , where p k p_{k} is the prompt
of length P k P_{k} and r k r_{k} is the raw response of length ℓ k \ell_{k} .
Over the IT data distribution, let ℓ \ell denote the raw response length before the
terminal [EOS] , and define its CDF as F ⁡ ( m ) = Pr ⁡ ( ℓ ≤ m ) F(m)=\Pr(\ell\leq m) .
After appending one semantic terminator [EOS] , each full sequence is padded with
 [EOS] to the maximum full-sequence length in the mini-batch, S B = max 1 ≤ k ≤ b ⁡ ( P k + ℓ k + 1 ) S_{B}=\max_{1\leq k\leq b}(P_{k}+\ell_{k}+1) .
Thus, example k k has padded response y k = r k ⊕ [EOS] ⊕ [EOS] L B , k − ℓ k − 1 y_{k}=r_{k}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}^{L_{B,k}-\ell_{k}-1} of length L B , k = S B − P k L_{B,k}=S_{B}-P_{k} .
During IT, both noise and loss are applied only to y k y_{k} . 
 
 
 
 
 3 Analysis of [EOS] Overflow 

 
 [EOS] overflow is a common issue of MDLM inference under large block sizes  ( 28 ) .
In a typical case, the model produces a very short answer followed by repeated [EOS] tokens, as shown in Figure  3 (a).
However, [EOS] overflow is not observed in ARLMs, even though both ARLMs and MDLMs use [EOS] as padding tokens during IT.
This motivates us to ask:
Why is [EOS] padding safe in ARLMs but fragile in MDLMs? 
 
 
 
 Observation 1: [EOS] overflow is not caused by limited large-block generation capacity. 
 
 
 
 We first test whether [EOS] overflow is caused by insufficient large-block generation capacity.
Specifically, we ban [EOS] commitment in LLaDA-8B-Instruct by setting the [EOS] logit to − ∞ -\infty throughout decoding, and then evaluate on GSM8K  15 with L = B = 512 L=B=512 .
Although this removes the semantic stopping signal and often causes repeated reasoning traces and answers, it raises the accuracy of the first extracted answer from 28.43% to 70.66%.
Thus, the model is not inherently unable to generate large blocks.

padding [EOS] tokens from earlier positions, so [EOS] remains
a semantic terminator and overflow does not arise.

 4 Method 

 
 Motivated by this training–inference role mismatch, we propose
 VoidPadding , which introduces a new [VOID] token as the
padding token, so that [EOS] serves only as the semantic terminator.
We further design VoidExpansion for inference, which uses the
 [VOID] length signal learned during IT to expand an insufficient
canvas before decoding.
Together, this training–inference co-design decouples padding from semantic
termination while preserving the hidden length signal carried by
 [VOID] tokens.
Figure  1 provides an overview of the method, with
details given in the following subsections. 
 
 
 4.1 VoidPadding 

 
 Instruction-Tuning Design. VoidPadding follows the IT data construction
described in Section  2.2 , except that
padding [EOS] tokens are replaced by [VOID] tokens. Thus, for
 B = { ( p k , r k ) } k = 1 b B=\{(p_{k},r_{k})\}_{k=1}^{b} and L B , k = S B − P k L_{B,k}=S_{B}-P_{k} , the padded response is 
 

 
 
 y k VOID = r k ⊕ [EOS] ⊕ [VOID] L B , k − ℓ k − 1 . y_{k}^{\mathrm{VOID}}=r_{k}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}\oplus\texttt{[VOID]}^{L_{B,k}-\ell_{k}-1}. 
 
 
 We then follow the standard MDLM training procedure in
Section  2.1 , with noise injection and loss
computation performed on y k VOID y_{k}^{\mathrm{VOID}} .
Notably, we apply standard MDLM masking for non- [EOS] positions.
The single [EOS] token in each example is always masked, forcing the
model to predict it as a stable semantic terminator. 
 
 
 Inference Design. 
During inference, [VOID] remains a hidden padding signal and is never
generated: setting its logit to − ∞ -\infty before token selection prevents the
high-confidence [VOID] tail from becoming visible output.
We also propose [EOS] Termination , which stops decoding once
all positions before the leftmost visible [EOS] are unmasked and then
discards the rest of the canvas.
In contrast, VanillaStopping , the standard MDLM stopping rule, stops
only after all masked positions are decoded
(Section  2.1 ), causing redundant decoding
after the semantic endpoint.

 5 Experiments 

 
 Setup. 
Our main experiments use two instruction-tuned MDLMs: LLaDA-8B-Instruct (hereafter LLaDA)  ( 38 ) and Dream-7B-Instruct (hereafter Dream)  ( 56 ) . Quality metrics are accuracy on
GSM8K  ( 15 ) and MATH500  ( 20 ) ,
and pass@1 on HumanEval  ( 10 ) and
MBPP  ( 5 ) .
The main efficiency metric is the average number of forward
passes per example (hereafter NFE).
In all tables, bold marks the best

 7 Related Work 

 
 Diffusion Language Models. 
Diffusion language models (DLMs) can be broadly categorized into continuous DLMs  ( 17 ; 24 ; 27 ; 43 ) , which operate in embedding spaces, and discrete DLMs  ( 22 ; 42 ; 16 ) , which operate over discrete token spaces.
Masked diffusion language models  ( 21 ; 19 ; 4 ) (MDLMs) are a representative discrete class that iteratively predicts masked tokens.
Recent large-scale MDLMs, including LLaDA series  38 ; 57 ; 8 ; 7 , Dream series  56 ; 52 , SDAR  12 , TraDo  47 , and WeDLM  34 , show strong complex reasoning and long-text generation abilities.
Recent work also improves generation quality through better samplers  ( 54 ) . 
 
 
 Efficient MDLMs. 
Although MDLMs support parallel token prediction, inference remains costly because generation usually requires many denoising iterations with full-sequence bidirectional attention. Work on efficient MDLM inference includes sampling methods that reduce denoising steps or finalize more tokens per step  ( 33 ; 26 ; 49 ) , cache-based methods that reuse computation via approximate caching or KV caching  50 ; 25 ; 45 ; 51 ; 36 ; 48 , and training-based distillation for fewer-step generation  ( 37 ; 2 ; 11 ) . 
 
 
 Variable-Length Generation. 
MDLMs often require a preallocated output canvas, wasting computation on over-allocated slots and limiting usability  ( 18 ) . Length allocation methods address this issue  13 . Inference-only methods, including SmartCrop  40 , ρ \rho - [EOS]   53 , and DAEDAL  32 , typically use [EOS] as a length signal, while training-based methods include dLLM-Var  ( 55 ) , which learns reliable [EOS] prediction for block-wise termination, and FlexMDM  ( 29 ) , which decouples length prediction from token prediction. 
 
 
 
 8 Conclusion 

 
 We show that a root cause of [EOS] overflow in MDLMs is a
training–inference role mismatch: [EOS] is trained as both padding
and semantic terminator, but used only for termination at inference.
VoidPadding instead uses [VOID] as padding tokens and reserves
 [EOS] for semantic endings, enabling VoidExpansion for canvas
allocation and [EOS] Termination for early stopping.
Together, our training–inference co-design improves large-block robustness,
decoding efficiency, and variable-length generation. 
 
 
 
 Limitations 

 
 Many MDLM training and inference conventions are inherited from ARLMs.
Our study focuses on one concrete failure mode of this transfer:
 [EOS] padding, which is largely benign under causal masking, can
become fragile under bidirectional denoising because padding positions can
serve as both context and denoising targets.
Thus, [EOS] overflow should be viewed as one symptom of a broader
class of ARLM-inherited assumptions that may fail in MDLMs.
Other choices, including data formatting, special-token semantics, stopping
rules, and batching strategies, may require similarly MDLM-specific design. 
 
 
 Our analysis also suggests that [EOS] overflow is not primarily a model
capacity failure. Instead, it emerges from the interaction between a learned
length prior, bidirectional context, and standard large-block sampling rules.
VoidPadding mitigates the padding–termination mismatch, but the design of
effective MDLM samplers remains open.
Future samplers may need to jointly consider token confidence, commitment
timing, canvas allocation, and stopping decisions under bidirectional denoising.

 Appendix A Training Configurations 

 
 Dataset. 

 
 We use the same 0.5M-example instruction-tuning dataset as the official
RainbowPadding  ( 28 ) implementation. The data pool combines Tulu3  ( 31 ) 
and SmolLM2  ( 1 ) . After removing examples longer than 4096
tokens and multi-turn dialogues, we randomly sample 0.5M examples and use this
identical data for all models in our experiments. 
 
 
 
 Shared training setup. 

 
 Following the official RainbowPadding training setup, we train LLaDA-8B-Instruct  ( 38 ) and Dream-7B-Instruct  ( 56 ) for the main
experiments, and use LLaDA-8B-Base and Dream-7B-Base for additional diagnostic
runs. All models are finetuned with LoRA  ( 23 ) of rank 32 applied
to all linear layers, without bias terms. We use AdamW with learning rate
 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} and global batch size 48. LLaDA models are trained on two
A800-80GB GPUs with mini-batch size 6 per GPU and 4 gradient-accumulation
steps; Dream models are trained on three A800-80GB GPUs with mini-batch size 4
per GPU and 4 gradient-accumulation steps. For both models, one epoch takes approximately
32–34 A800 GPU hours. 
 
 
 
 Training budgets. 

 
 RainbowPadding follows its official budget: one epoch for LLaDA-8B-Instruct and
Dream-7B-Instruct, corresponding to 10,417 optimizer updates, and three epochs
for LLaDA-8B-Base and Dream-7B-Base. All VoidPadding checkpoints use 67% of
the corresponding RainbowPadding training budget: 7000 optimizer updates for
LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream-7B-Instruct, and two epochs for LLaDA-8B-Base and
Dream-7B-Base. The [EOS] -padding diagnostic models are trained for two
epochs on LLaDA-8B-Base and Dream-7B-Base. 
 
 
 
 67% training-budget comparison. 

 
 Figures  14 and
 14 compare Early RainbowPadding and
VoidPadding under the same training budget. Specifically, Early RainbowPadding
uses 7000 optimizer updates, matching the VoidPadding training budget and
corresponding to 67% of the official RainbowPadding budget for both
LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream-7B-Instruct. All other settings are kept identical,
including the training data, LoRA configuration, optimizer, batch size, fixed
generation length, and the absence of VoidExpansion, matching the fixed-length
generation robustness experiments in Table  1 .
Under this matched setup,
VoidPadding achieves better accuracy than Early RainbowPadding on
both models.
